Queen's Relectric · APSC 103 design project · Jan – Apr 2023

PID Battery Temperature Regulation

A closed-loop cooling controller for the battery enclosure of a 1997 Jeep TJ being converted to fully electric. Thermistors measure battery temperature and an Arduino PID loop sets the speed of five PWM fans, so the system cools only as hard as it needs to.

The brief

Keep the pack cool without wasting power

Queen's Relectric needed a battery enclosure that actively regulates battery temperature and protects the cells. My team built a scaled-down, four-battery plywood prototype with four intake fans, one exhaust fan and two thermistors per battery.

I owned the software side: reading the sensors, converting them to temperature, and closing the loop with a PID controller that adjusts fan speed automatically instead of running the fans flat out all the time.

Sizing the cooling

Worst-case heat vs fan capacity

Battery heat comes mostly from resistive loss in the cells, so we sized the fans against the worst-case current draw.

How it works

The control loop

fan = Kp·e + Ki·Σe·dt + Kd·de/dt,   e = Tmeasured − Tsetpoint,   clamped to 0–100 %

P · present

The hotter the pack is above setpoint, the faster the fans spin. On its own it settles slightly above setpoint, because it needs some error to keep the fans running.

I · past

Accumulates lingering error and keeps pushing fan speed up until that offset is gone. Clamped when the fans are maxed out to prevent integral windup.

D · future

Reacts to how fast temperature is changing, ramping the fans early on a fast rise. Kept small, since thermistor noise looks like rapid change.

Results

Testing the prototype

  • A 75 °C heat source placed in the enclosure cooled to 30 °C in 19 minutes with the system running, vs 60 minutes passively: about 3× faster
  • With a powered light bulb as a continuous heat source, the controller held temperature within ±5 °C of setpoint
  • Delivered the prototype for $97.50 against a $100 budget

What I'd do differently

Tuning it properly

We tuned the gains by hand, logging temperature and fan speed every second and adjusting Kp, Ki and Kd based on the response. It worked, but it wasn't optimal. Today I would:

  1. Run a step test to measure the enclosure's time constant and sensor delay

  2. Use Ziegler–Nichols to get starting gains, then fine-tune for overshoot, settling time and steady-state error

  3. Likely drop to PI with anti-windup, since thermal systems are slow and D amplifies sensor noise

  4. Test with a dynamic heat load, since a single hot source mostly measures raw cooling power
